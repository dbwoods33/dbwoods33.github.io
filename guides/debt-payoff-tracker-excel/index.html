Debt snowball vs avalanche: which pays off faster?

The avalanche method is mathematically cheaper. The snowball method gets your first win much sooner. Here is exactly how big the difference is on a realistic set of debts, and how to decide.

The short version

  • Avalanche (highest interest rate first) always costs the least interest. In our example it saves $429.69 and finishes one month sooner.
  • Snowball (smallest balance first) delivers the first paid-off debt in month 3 instead of month 16.
  • Either method beats minimum payments by a wide margin: 26 or 25 months instead of 56, and less than half the interest.
  • What matters most is the total you pay each month and rolling freed-up payments into the next debt. Pick the method you will stick with.

Both methods share the same core: pay the minimum on every debt, throw every extra dollar at one target debt, and when that debt is gone, roll its payment into the next one. The only difference is the order.

  • Debt snowball: target the smallest balance first, regardless of interest rate.
  • Debt avalanche: target the highest interest rate (APR) first, regardless of balance.

Arguments about which is “better” are usually abstract. So let’s run both on the same debts, month by month, and look at the actual numbers.

The example: four debts and $905 a month

Imagine someone with $19,800 of debt spread across four accounts. Their minimums add up to $605 a month, and they can find an extra $300, so they commit $905 a month to debt until it’s all gone.

Notice that the two methods disagree about where to start. The store card has the smallest balance (snowball’s pick), but the credit card has the highest rate (avalanche’s pick). If your smallest debt also had your highest rate, both methods would choose the same order and produce identical results.

How the calculation works

Each month, interest is added to every balance at APR ÷ 12, rounded to the cent. Then every debt gets its minimum payment, and whatever is left of the $905 goes to the target debt. When a debt is paid off, its minimum stays in the $905 and flows to the next target. That roll-over is what makes both methods accelerate.

Month 1, side by side

Snowball: the store card accrues $14.00 of interest ($800 × 21% ÷ 12), taking it to $814.00. It gets its $30 minimum plus the full $300 extra, $330 in all, leaving $484.00. The other three debts get their minimums.

Avalanche: the credit card accrues $130.00 ($6,500 × 24% ÷ 12), taking it to $6,630.00. It gets $195 plus the $300 extra, $495 in all, leaving $6,135.00.

The snowball has already cut a balance by about 40%. The avalanche has barely dented its target, but that target is the one charging $130 a month in interest.

Avalanche saves $429.69 in interest and finishes one month sooner. Paying only the minimums, with nothing extra and no roll-over, would take 56 months and cost $5,996.29 in interest.

So the avalanche wins on cost, as it always will: by attacking the 24% card first, it shrinks the most expensive balance soonest. But look at the payoff pattern. The snowballer closes an account in month 3 and another in month 10. The avalanche user waits until month 16 for their first payoff, more than a year of sending money in with no account closed.

In this example the price of those early wins is $429.69 over roughly two years, or about $16.53 a month. That is real money, but it’s also small compared with the $3,130.38 the snowball saves versus paying minimums.

When the gap gets bigger, or disappears

The difference between the methods depends on your debts:

  • The gap grows when your largest balance also has your highest rate, for example a big credit card at 25% and a small loan at 5%. Snowball leaves the expensive balance compounding for longer.
  • The gap shrinks when interest rates are close together, or when your smallest balances happen to carry your highest rates.
  • The gap is zero when both rules give the same order.
  • Extra payment size matters more than order. Raising the extra payment from $300 to $400 would change the result far more than switching methods.

Run your own numbers before you decide. If the avalanche saves $40, the motivation of the snowball is probably worth it. If it saves $4,000, it probably isn’t.

Which should you choose?

Choose the avalanche if you are motivated by numbers, your high-rate debts are large, or the interest gap is significant. Choose the snowball if you’ve tried before and lost momentum, you have several small balances you could clear quickly, or the interest difference is small.

There is research behind the snowball’s appeal. A 2012 study by David Gal and Blakeley McShane in the Journal of Marketing Research found that consumers who closed more individual accounts were more likely to go on to eliminate their debt, even after accounting for the amounts involved. Progress you can see seems to keep people going.

A practical hybrid: clear any very small balances first for a quick win, then switch to avalanche order for the rest.

Rules that matter more than the method

  • Keep the monthly total fixed. When a debt is paid off, its payment moves to the next debt. It does not become spending money.
  • Stop adding new debt. Neither method works if balances keep growing behind you.
  • Keep a small emergency fund so a car repair doesn’t go straight back on a card.
  • Watch promotional rates. A 0% balance transfer that ends in six months should be treated by the rate it will become.
  • Never miss a minimum. Late fees and penalty rates undo months of progress.

How to track it in a spreadsheet

You need three things: a list of debts (balance, APR, minimum), a payment log, and a month-by-month simulation for each strategy. The simulation is the tricky part. Each row needs to add interest, apply minimums, apply the extra, and roll over freed-up payments, which is where homemade spreadsheets usually break. Online calculators handle the maths but forget your progress the moment you close the tab.

Frequently asked questions

Which is faster, snowball or avalanche?

With the same monthly payment, the avalanche method is never slower and never costs more interest, because it always targets the most expensive balance. In practice the difference is often small, sometimes only a month or less.

Why do people choose the debt snowball if it costs more?

Because paying off a whole account early is motivating, and motivation is what keeps people paying for years. Research on consumer debt has found that closing individual accounts is linked to a higher chance of eliminating debt altogether.

Should I include my mortgage in the snowball?

Most people leave the mortgage out and focus on consumer debts like credit cards, personal loans and car loans. Mortgages usually have lower rates and much longer terms.

What if two debts have the same interest rate?

Pay the smaller balance first. It frees up its minimum payment sooner, which helps either method.

How much extra should I pay each month?

As much as you can sustain without new borrowing. In the example, $300 extra cut the payoff time from 56 months to about 25 and saved more than $3,000 in interest compared with minimums.

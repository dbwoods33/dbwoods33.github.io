How to make a monthly budget in Excel, step by step

A free, formula-by-formula method that takes about an hour to set up and fifteen minutes a week to run, plus the mistakes that make most spreadsheet budgets quietly stop working.

The short version

  • A working budget needs only two tabs: a Budget tab with planned amounts and a Transactions tab where you log what you spend.
  • One formula does the heavy lifting: SUMIFS adds up each category’s spending for the month you pick.
  • Give every dollar of take-home pay a job (zero-based), then sanity-check the split against 50/30/20.
  • The budget only works if you log spending at least weekly. Make that as easy as possible with drop-downs.

Most people who try to budget in Excel start with a blank grid, type a list of bills, and give up by the second month. The problem is rarely the maths. It is that the spreadsheet only records a plan, so it can never tell you whether you stuck to it.

A budget that works has two halves: what you planned to spend in each category, and what you actually spent. This guide builds both in plain Excel (or Google Sheets), with formulas you can copy. No macros, no add-ins and no paid software.

What a monthly budget needs

Strip away the colours and charts and every budget spreadsheet is answering four questions:

  1. How much came in? Your take-home pay, after tax and deductions, plus any side income.
  2. Where should it go? A planned amount for each category.
  3. Where did it actually go? Real transactions, each tagged with a category.
  4. What’s the gap? Planned minus actual, by category and in total.

Budget from take-home pay, not salary. If your paycheck lands as $4,200 a month after tax, that is the number you have to spend, whatever your offer letter says.

Step 1: Set up two tabs

Create a new workbook with two sheets named Budget and Transactions. Keeping the plan and the log separate is what makes the whole thing maintainable: you never edit formulas when you add spending, you just add rows.

On Transactions, type four headers in row 1: Date, Description, Category, Amount. Click any header and press Ctrl+T (⌘+T on a Mac) to turn the range into an Excel Table and name it Transactions under Table Design. Tables grow automatically, so formulas pointing at them never need updating.

On Budget, put the month you are tracking in cell B1 as a date on the first of the month (for example 1/11/2026 or 11/1/2026, depending on your regional date format). Then add headers in row 4: Category, Planned, Actual, Left.

Step 2: Plan your categories

List your categories in column A from row 5 down. Fifteen to twenty is plenty; more than that and logging becomes a chore. Then fill in Planned using one of two approaches.

Zero-based budgeting

Assign every dollar of income to a category, including savings and debt, until income minus the plan equals zero. Money doesn’t disappear into “whatever’s left”, because nothing is left unassigned.

The 50/30/20 check

The 50/30/20 rule suggests roughly 50% of take-home pay on needs, 30% on wants and 20% on savings and extra debt payments. It is a useful smell test rather than a law. Group your categories and see how far off you are:

To calculate a group total, add a Group column next to your categories and use =SUMIFS(B:B, E:E, "Needs"), adjusting column letters to match your sheet. Divide by income for the percentage.

Step 3: Log transactions with drop-downs

Typos break budgets: “Groceries” and “Grocery” are two different categories to a formula. Stop them with a drop-down list.

  1. Select the Category column of your Transactions table.
  2. Go to Data → Data Validation (in Google Sheets: Data → Data validation → Dropdown (from a range)).
  3. Choose List and set the source to your category list, for example =Budget!$A$5:$A$24.

Now each new row offers your exact category names. Enter spending as positive numbers. If you also want to track income here, add income categories such as “Salary” to the same list.

Log in whichever way you’ll keep doing: daily from receipts, or weekly from your banking app. Most banks also let you export a CSV you can paste in and categorise.

Step 4: Calculate actual spending with SUMIFS

This is the formula that turns a list of transactions into a budget report. In C5 on the Budget tab, enter:

=SUMIFS(Transactions[Amount], Transactions[Category], A5,
        Transactions[Date], ">="&$B$1,
        Transactions[Date], "<"&EDATE($B$1,1))

In plain English: add up every amount whose category matches A5 and whose date falls on or after the first of the selected month but before the first of the next month. EDATE($B$1,1) moves the date forward exactly one month, so it handles 28-, 30- and 31-day months without any special cases. Copy the formula down for every category.

Google Sheets understands the same function, but if your sheet doesn’t use Table references, point at plain columns instead:

=SUMIFS(Transactions!$D:$D, Transactions!$C:$C, A5,
        Transactions!$A:$A, ">="&$B$1,
        Transactions!$A:$A, "<"&EDATE($B$1,1))

Change B1 to a different month and every Actual figure recalculates. That single cell is your month selector.

Step 5: Show what’s left and flag overspending

In D5, subtract actual from planned and copy down:

=B5-C5

Add a status column if you want a quick read:

=IF(C5>B5,"Over",IF(C5>=0.9*B5,"Close","OK"))

Then use Home → Conditional Formatting → Highlight Cells Rules → Less Than 0 on the Left column to turn overspending red. At the bottom, total each column with SUM and add two numbers worth watching every month:

  • Money left this month: total income minus total actual spending.
  • Savings rate: money left (plus anything you moved to savings categories) divided by income. Even 10% is a solid start.

Step 6: A 15-minute weekly routine

The spreadsheet is the easy part. The habit is what makes it work. Once a week:

  1. Log or paste in the week’s transactions and pick categories.
  2. Scan the Left column. Anything red or close to zero?
  3. Decide, don’t just observe: move money from a category with slack, or slow down spending in the one that’s running hot.

At the end of the month, copy the Planned column into next month (or keep one Budget tab and change B1), adjust for anything you learned, and start again. Expect the first two or three months to be rough. Your plan gets more accurate every month because it is built on what you actually spend.

Common mistakes that break spreadsheet budgets

  • Budgeting from gross salary. Tax and deductions vanish before you see the money. Always plan from take-home pay.
  • Forgetting irregular expenses. Car registration, annual subscriptions and gifts don’t arrive monthly, but they still need money each month. Add a sinking-funds line and set aside 1/12 of the yearly cost.
  • Too many categories. If you can’t decide between two categories while logging, merge them.
  • Hard-coding numbers into formulas. Keep inputs (planned amounts, the month) in cells and point formulas at them.
  • Treating overspending as failure. A red cell is information. Adjust the plan or the behaviour and keep going.

Frequently asked questions

Does Excel have a free budget template?

Yes. Excel and Google Sheets both include basic budget templates under File → New. They are fine for a simple plan, but most don’t track actual transactions by month, which is the part that keeps you accountable. The two-tab method in this guide is also free and does.

Should I use Excel or Google Sheets for a budget?

Either works with the formulas above. Google Sheets is free, runs in a browser and syncs to your phone, which makes logging on the go easier. Excel is faster with large files and works offline. Pick the one you will actually open every week.

What is the best budgeting method for beginners?

Zero-based budgeting is the most effective for most beginners because it forces a decision about every dollar. Use 50/30/20 as a quick check on whether your overall split is reasonable.

How many budget categories should I have?

Between 12 and 20 works for most households. Fewer than that and you can’t see where money leaks. More than that and logging takes too long, so you stop.

How do I handle income that changes every month?

Budget from your lowest typical month. When you earn more, send the extra to savings or a buffer category first, then use it to smooth out the leaner months.

How to keep a trading journal: the metrics that matter

A trading journal turns a pile of wins and losses into evidence. Here’s what to log on every trade, and the handful of numbers that tell you whether your approach has an edge.

The short version

  • Log every closed trade: date, instrument, side, setup, entry, stop, exit, size, fees, and a note on whether you followed your plan.
  • Measure results in R: profit or loss divided by the amount you risked. It makes trades of different sizes comparable.
  • Win rate means little alone. Expectancy combines win rate with average win and loss size to show the average result per trade.
  • Judge a setup on dozens of trades, not a handful, and review by setup and by mistake tag.
Educational onlyThis guide explains record-keeping and performance arithmetic. It is not investment advice and doesn’t recommend any strategy, security or position size. Trading involves substantial risk of loss.

Most traders remember their best trades and their worst ones. The hundreds in between, the ones that decide whether an approach works, blur together. A trading journal fixes that by writing everything down at the time, so you can later measure what actually happened instead of what it felt like.

This guide covers what to record, how to calculate the core performance metrics by hand or in a spreadsheet, and how to review your journal so it changes what you do next.

What to record on every trade

Record each trade when you close it, while the reasoning is fresh. The essentials:

  • Date and instrument (ticker, pair or contract)
  • Side: long or short
  • Setup: the named pattern or reason for the trade, chosen from a short fixed list
  • Entry price, stop price and exit price
  • Quantity and fees
  • Tag: did you follow your plan, or was it FOMO, an early exit, a moved stop, an oversized position?
  • Notes: one line on what you saw and what you’d do differently

The stop price is the one people skip, and it’s the one you need most. Without it you can’t calculate risk, and without risk you can’t calculate R.

Keep setups and tags to a fixed drop-down list. Free-typed labels (“breakout”, “Breakout”, “BO”) split your statistics into meaningless pieces.

R-multiple: measure every trade in units of risk

R is the amount you planned to lose if the trade hit your stop. Every result is then expressed as a multiple of that risk:

Risk (1R)   = |Entry − Stop| × Quantity
R-multiple  = P/L ÷ Risk

Example: you buy 100 shares at $50 with a stop at $48. Your risk is $2 × 100 = $200. You sell at $55 for a $500 gain, which is +2.5R. Had the stop been hit, the loss would have been −$200, or −1R.

R matters because it makes trades comparable. A $500 win means one thing on a $200 risk and something else on a $2,000 risk. It also exposes discipline problems: losses much bigger than −1R mean stops weren’t honoured.

In a spreadsheet, with entry in F2, exit in G2, stop in H2, quantity in E2, fees in I2 and side in C2:

P/L:  =IF(C2="Short", F2-G2, G2-F2)*E2 - I2
R:    =IFERROR(J2/(ABS(F2-H2)*E2), "")

Here J2 is the P/L cell. Because fees are subtracted from P/L, R reflects your net result.

Win rate

The percentage of trades that made money. It’s the number people quote most and the least useful on its own. A 40% win rate is excellent if winners are much bigger than losers, and a 70% win rate can lose money if the occasional loss is huge.

Expectancy

The average amount you make or lose per trade. You can calculate it two equivalent ways: win rate × average win − loss rate × average loss, or simply total P/L ÷ number of trades. Here both give +0.2R, or $40 per trade (+2R ÷ 10 = 0.2R). Positive expectancy over a meaningful sample is what “having an edge” means in numbers.

Profit factor

Gross profit divided by gross loss: $1,500 ÷ $1,100 = 1.36. Anything above 1 is profitable before costs; below 1 is losing.

Break-even win rate

Given your average win and loss, the win rate at which expectancy is zero. With wins averaging 1.875R and losses 0.917R, you break even at 32.8%. At 40% this sample is above it. If your average win shrinks, the break-even rate rises. This is a useful way to see how much room for error you have.

Drawdown and the equity curve

Add a running equity column: starting balance plus the cumulative sum of P/L. Charting it shows the path your account actually took, which matters as much as where it ended.

Maximum drawdown is the largest fall from a peak to a later low. If equity climbed to $27,400 and then fell to $25,900 before recovering, the drawdown was $1,500, or 5.5% of the peak. Large or long drawdowns are where traders abandon sound plans, so knowing your typical drawdown in advance helps you stick to one.

Slice your results to find what works

Overall stats tell you whether you’re profitable. Breakdowns tell you why. Calculate trades, win rate, total P/L and average R by:

  • Setup: often one or two setups carry the results and another quietly loses money.
  • Side: long vs short.
  • Day of week or time of day.
  • Tag: compare “followed plan” trades with FOMO, revenge or oversized trades. This is usually the most eye-opening view.

COUNTIFS, SUMIFS and AVERAGEIFS do all of this from the trade log. For example, average R for one setup: =AVERAGEIFS(R_col, Setup_col, "Breakout").

Be careful with small samples. Ten trades, like the example above, prove nothing; a few lucky outcomes can swing every number. Look for patterns that persist across dozens of trades per setup before you change your approach.

A weekly review routine

  1. Make sure every closed trade is logged with its stop, setup and tag.
  2. Check the week’s P/L in R, win rate and largest loss. Was any loss worse than −1R? Why?
  3. Review trades tagged as anything other than “followed plan”. What triggered them?
  4. Monthly, compare setups. Is there one you should stop trading, or trade smaller?
  5. Write one rule for next week, and only one.

The goal of a journal isn’t to admire statistics. It’s to make one specific change at a time and see whether the numbers improve.

Frequently asked questions

What should be in a trading journal?

At minimum: date, instrument, side, setup, entry, stop, exit, quantity, fees and a tag for whether you followed your plan. The stop price is essential, because it defines your risk and lets you calculate R-multiples.

What is an R-multiple in trading?

An R-multiple is a trade’s profit or loss divided by the amount risked on it, where risk is the distance from entry to stop multiplied by position size. A trade that makes twice what it risked is +2R.

How do you calculate trading expectancy?

Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss). It equals total profit or loss divided by the number of trades, and can be expressed in dollars or in R.

What is a good win rate for trading?

There isn’t one on its own. Whether a win rate is good depends on the size of your average win relative to your average loss. A 35–40% win rate can be profitable with large winners; a high win rate can lose money with occasional large losses.

What is profit factor?

Profit factor is gross profit divided by gross loss over a set of trades. Above 1 means the trades made more than they lost; below 1 means they lost money overall.

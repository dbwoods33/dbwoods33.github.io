Budgeting

How to budget by paycheck, including biweekly pay

If your money arrives every two weeks but your bills arrive monthly, a monthly budget can say you’re fine while your account says otherwise. Here’s how to budget the way you actually get paid.

The short version

  • Assign each bill to the paycheck that arrives before its due date, not to the calendar month.
  • If one paycheck carries most of the big bills (usually rent), set part of it aside from the other paycheck.
  • Biweekly pay means 26 paychecks a year. Budget on two per month and the two extra paychecks become free money for goals.
  • The long-term goal is a one-paycheck buffer, so this month’s bills are paid from last month’s income.

Monthly budgets assume all your income arrives at once and all your bills wait politely until it does. Real life is lumpier. Rent is due on the 1st, your paycheck lands on the 9th, and the car payment on the 5th has to come out of money that arrived two weeks ago.

Paycheck budgeting solves this by planning each paycheck separately: which bills it pays, how much it saves, and what’s left to live on until the next one. It is especially useful if you are paid every other week, or if your account runs close to zero before payday.

Biweekly vs semi-monthly: know your schedule

“Twice a month” and “every two weeks” sound the same, but they produce different numbers of paychecks:

Biweekly paychecks are smaller than semi-monthly ones for the same salary, because the year is split 26 ways instead of 24. The trade-off is that in two months of the year you get a third paycheck. (Depending on how the calendar falls, a few years contain 27 biweekly paydays.)

The safe way to budget biweekly pay is to plan every month on two paychecks. In the example above that’s $3,700 a month, not the $4,008.33 monthly average. The extra paychecks then become a bonus you can plan for, not money you have already spent.

Step 1: List every bill with its due date

Write down every fixed bill with its amount and due day: rent, loans, insurance, phone, utilities, subscriptions, minimum card payments. Your bank statement and email receipts from the last two months will catch the ones you forget. Note which are on autopay, because those happen whether or not the money is there.

Step 2: Assign each bill to a paycheck

The rule is simple: each bill is paid by the most recent paycheck before its due date. In this example the paychecks land around the 9th and the 23rd, so Paycheck A covers bills due from the 9th to the 22nd, and Paycheck B covers the 23rd through the 8th of the following month.

In a spreadsheet you can automate this. List your actual pay dates in a column (each one is the previous date plus 14 for biweekly pay), then find the paycheck that covers a bill’s due date with:

=XLOOKUP(DueDate, PayDates, PayDates, , -1)

The -1 tells XLOOKUP to return the closest pay date on or before the due date. XLOOKUP is available in Excel 2021, Microsoft 365 and Google Sheets. In older Excel, =LOOKUP(DueDate, PayDates) does the same job, provided the pay dates are sorted oldest first.

Step 3: Balance the two halves of the month

Now total each paycheck’s bills. This is where most people discover the problem:

Total left across both paychecks is unchanged at $1,280; balancing just stops one half of the month from going negative.

Paycheck B carries rent, the car payment and three other bills, so it is $275 short, while Paycheck A has plenty left. The fix isn’t more money; it’s timing. Paycheck A sets aside half the rent and pays the car payment early, and both halves of the month become workable.

Moving money between paychecks works best with a separate “bills” account or savings bucket: transfer the set-aside amount as soon as Paycheck A lands so it can’t be spent by accident.

Step 4: Give the rest of each paycheck a job

After bills, each paycheck still has to cover the next two weeks of living. Split what’s left before you spend any of it, in this order:

  1. Savings first. Even a small fixed amount per paycheck, moved on payday.
  2. Essentials until next payday: groceries, fuel or transit, medicine.
  3. Everything else: eating out, fun, small purchases.

Here Paycheck A has $535 and Paycheck B has $745 left. If $150 goes to savings from each, that leaves $385 and $595 for two weeks of groceries, fuel and everything else. Seeing that number on payday is far more useful than a monthly total that hides when the money runs out.

Step 5: Plan your three-paycheck months

On a biweekly schedule, two months a year have a third paycheck. Because your bills are already covered by the first two, that third paycheck is unassigned. In the example it is $1,850, or $3,700 across the year. Decide in advance where it goes:

  • Build a one-paycheck buffer (see below).
  • Make an extra debt payment.
  • Top up sinking funds for irregular costs like car repairs or holidays.

Mark the months on your calendar. Look up your actual pay dates rather than guessing, since they shift by a day or two each month.

Step 6: Work toward a one-paycheck buffer

Paycheck budgeting is a great way to stop overdrafts, but the long-term goal is to stop needing it. Once you hold one paycheck’s worth of money in your checking account as a buffer, you can pay this month’s bills with money that arrived last month. Due dates stop mattering, and you can switch to a simple monthly budget.

The fastest route is usually a mix of your extra paychecks and the balancing trick in Step 3. Many people get there in six to twelve months.

Frequently asked questions

How do I budget if I get paid every two weeks?

Plan each month on two paychecks, assign each bill to the paycheck that arrives before its due date, and treat the two extra paychecks a year as bonus money for savings or debt.

What is the difference between biweekly and semi-monthly pay?

Biweekly means every other week: 26 paychecks a year, on a different date each month. Semi-monthly means twice a month on fixed dates such as the 1st and 15th: 24 paychecks a year.

Which months have three paychecks?

It depends on your first payday of the year. If you’re paid biweekly, count forward 14 days at a time from your first payday in January; the months that contain three dates are your three-paycheck months. Most years have two.

What should I do with a third paycheck?

Since your bills are already covered, put it toward a one-paycheck buffer first, then debt or sinking funds. Decide before it arrives so it isn’t absorbed into everyday spending.

Is paycheck-to-paycheck budgeting a bad sign?

No. Budgeting by paycheck is a timing tool, and it works at any income. It is most helpful while you build a buffer, after which many people switch to a monthly budget.

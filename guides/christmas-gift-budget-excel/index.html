How to set a Christmas gift budget per person

The money that disappears at Christmas isn’t only gifts. It’s food, travel, wrapping and the extra present you didn’t plan for. Here’s how to set a total you can actually pay for, then divide it fairly person by person.

The short version

  • Set your total from what you can pay without new debt by Christmas, not from what last year cost.
  • Budget holiday extras (food, travel, decor, cards, wrapping) separately before you divide what’s left between people.
  • Group people into tiers and give each tier a per-person amount. Keep a small buffer for the gift you forgot.
  • Track each gift from idea to wrapped, with the real price including shipping.

Christmas spending has a way of happening in small, reasonable pieces: a gift here, a grocery run there, a last-minute present for the neighbour who surprised you. None of them feel large until the card statement arrives in January.

A Christmas budget fixes that by working the other way round: decide the total first, then split it. This guide shows how to choose a realistic number, divide it between gifts and everything else, set fair per-person amounts, and track it through December.

Step 1: Set a total you can pay for

The right Christmas budget isn’t what you spent last year or what others spend. It’s the amount you can pay for without borrowing. Work it out from three sources:

  • What you’ve already saved for the holidays, if anything.
  • What you can set aside from each paycheck between now and mid-December, after your normal bills and savings.
  • Any money you’ve deliberately earmarked, such as a bonus.

Add them up and that’s your ceiling. If it feels small, that’s useful information now rather than in January. In this guide’s example, the total is $1,200.

Next year, make it easy on yourself: $1,200 saved over twelve months is $100 a month. Saved over the last eight weeks, it’s $150 a week. A holiday sinking fund turns December into a planned expense instead of a shock.

Step 2: Budget the extras first

Gifts get all the attention, but the rest of the season can easily take a quarter or more of the total. List the extras and give them a number before you allocate a dollar to gifts:

That leaves $1,200 − $350 = $850 for gifts. If you host Christmas dinner or travel to family, your extras may be much larger, which is exactly why they come first.

Step 3: Set a budget per person

Write a list of everyone you plan to buy for, then group them into tiers: partner, children, close family, extended family and friends, and the people you buy small thank-you gifts for. Give each tier a per-person amount and multiply out.

The $20 buffer matters. There is always a gift you didn’t plan for, and it’s far better to have a small line ready for it than to raid someone else’s budget.

If the subtotals come to more than your gift budget, adjust in this order: trim the largest tiers slightly, reduce the number of people on the list (suggest a gift exchange), and only then cut across the board.

Ways to keep per-person costs down

  • Secret Santa or a gift exchange for large families and friend groups: one good gift each instead of many small ones.
  • The four-gift rule for children: something they want, something they need, something to wear and something to read.
  • Family gifts instead of individual gifts for households.
  • Homemade or experience gifts for tiers where the thought matters more than the price.

Step 4: Track every gift from idea to given

A simple gift list keeps you honest. For each gift, record who it’s for, the idea, the budgeted amount, the actual price including shipping, where it’s from and its status. A status pipeline (idea → ordered → bought → wrapped → given) means you can see at a glance what still needs buying and what’s still in transit.

In a spreadsheet, each person’s spending is =SUMIFS(Price, Person, "Mum"), and what’s left is their budget minus that. A conditional format that turns the cell red below zero does the rest.

Shipping is the most common leak. Two $8 delivery charges on a $40 budget line put you 40% over without buying anything extra.

Step 5: Shop with a list, not a feeling

  • Have your list before the sales. Black Friday and Cyber Monday are useful if you already know what you need. Without a list, they are just more things to buy.
  • Buy early for the people who are hard to shop for. Last-minute buying is when budgets break.
  • Count gift cards and cash as gifts in your tracker. They’re easy to forget because they don’t need wrapping.
  • Pay with money you’ve set aside. If you use a credit card for points or protection, pay it off in full from your holiday fund.

After Christmas: two minutes for next year

In early January, look at what you planned against what you spent, by tier and on extras. Note what surprised you. Then start next year’s holiday sinking fund with a monthly amount based on the real total. It’s the single best way to make next December cheaper.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I spend on Christmas gifts?

Spend what you can pay for without new debt. Start from your savings and what you can set aside before December, subtract holiday extras like food and travel, and divide the rest between the people on your list.

How do I set a Christmas budget per person?

Group people into tiers (partner, children, close family, friends, thank-you gifts), give each tier a per-person amount, and check the total fits your gift budget. Keep a small buffer for unplanned gifts.

What should a Christmas budget include besides gifts?

Food and hosting, travel, decorations, cards and postage, wrapping, and any parties or outings. Budget these first, because they can take a large share of the total.

When should I start saving for Christmas?

Ideally in January, as a monthly sinking fund. A $1,200 Christmas is $100 a month over a year, but $150 a week if you start eight weeks out.

How do I stop overspending on Christmas?

Write a list with per-person amounts before you shop, track the real price of every gift including shipping, and pay from money set aside rather than credit.

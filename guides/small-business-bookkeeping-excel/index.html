Simple bookkeeping for a small business, in a spreadsheet

If you’re a freelancer, sole proprietor or small shop, you don’t need accounting software on day one. You need a clean ledger, consistent categories, a monthly profit number and a tax pot that’s never short.

The short version

  • Use a separate business bank account. It halves the work and makes everything else possible.
  • Record every sale and expense in one ledger with a date, description, category, amount and a receipt tick-box.
  • Build a monthly profit & loss from the ledger with SUMIFS, so the numbers never need retyping.
  • Set aside a fixed share of each quarter’s profit for tax. Many sole proprietors use 25–30%, but confirm your rate with an accountant.

Bookkeeping has a reputation for being complicated, but for a small, simple business it comes down to three questions. How much came in? How much went out, and on what? And how much of what’s left belongs to the tax authority?

A well-built spreadsheet answers all three, and it’s a perfectly reasonable system for a freelancer, consultant, maker or small service business. This guide walks through a setup you can build in an afternoon and keep up in ten minutes a week.

NoteThis is general information, not tax or accounting advice. Tax rules and deadlines vary by country, state and business type. The tax examples use US federal estimated-tax dates; check the rules where you are, and talk to an accountant about your rate.

Step 1: Separate business and personal money

Open a bank account (and ideally a card) used only for the business. Every business sale lands there and every business expense leaves from it. When you need money for yourself, transfer it to your personal account as a single owner’s draw.

This one step does most of your bookkeeping for you. Your bank statement becomes a nearly complete list of business transactions, and you never have to pick through grocery receipts to find a software subscription.

Step 2: Keep one ledger for everything

Use a single sheet for all income and expenses. One row per transaction, with these columns:

  • Date
  • Description: “Invoice 1042 – logo design”, “Adobe subscription”
  • Client / vendor
  • Category: from a drop-down list (see below)
  • Method: bank, card, cash, PayPal, Stripe
  • Amount: always a positive number
  • Receipt: a tick when you’ve saved the receipt or invoice

Income and expenses are told apart by category, not by plus or minus signs, which avoids the most common spreadsheet error. A helper column can label each row automatically: =IF(COUNTIF(IncomeCats, D2)>0, "Income", "Expense"), where IncomeCats is the list of your income categories.

The receipt tick-box matters more than it looks. If you’re ever asked to support a deduction, a ledger that shows which receipts you have is far easier to work with than a shoebox.

Step 3: Choose categories that match your tax return

Pick a short list of income categories (services, product sales, other income) and expense categories, then stick to them. The best expense categories mirror the lines on the tax form you file. In the US, sole proprietors report on Schedule C, which has lines for advertising, contract labor, insurance, office expense, rent, supplies, travel, meals and more. Matching them means tax time is a matter of reading totals off your sheet.

Typical small-business expense categories: advertising, software and subscriptions, supplies, contractors, rent and utilities, travel, meals, fees and bank charges, equipment, insurance, and professional fees. Add an “Other” line, but if it grows, split it.

Step 4: Build a monthly profit & loss

A profit and loss statement (P&L) is simply revenue minus expenses for a period, broken down by category. With your ledger in place, each cell is one formula. For a category in A5 and a month starting on the date in C$4:

=SUMIFS(Ledger[Amount], Ledger[Category], $A5,
        Ledger[Date], ">="&C$4, Ledger[Date], "<"&EDATE(C$4,1))

Fill it across twelve months and down every category, then total revenue, total expenses and profit at the bottom. Here’s what a quarter might look like:

Watch two things month to month: profit margin (is each dollar of revenue keeping more or less profit?) and any expense category that jumps compared with the month before.

Step 5: Set aside tax every quarter

Employees have tax withheld from every paycheck. When you work for yourself, nobody does that for you, and the bill can be a shock. In the US, if you expect to owe at least $1,000 in federal tax for the year, the IRS generally expects estimated payments during the year.

The simplest habit: at the end of each quarter, calculate profit and move a fixed percentage of it to a separate tax savings account.

Why 25%? In the US, self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) is 15.3% on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings, roughly 14% of profit by itself. On $50,800 of profit that is about $7,177.81, before any income tax. Federal and state income tax come on top, so many sole proprietors set aside somewhere between 25% and 30%. Your right rate depends on your total income, deductions, filing status and location, so treat 25% as a placeholder until your accountant gives you a better number.

US federal estimated tax payments are normally due on 15 April, 15 June, 15 September and 15 January (moving to the next business day when a date falls on a weekend or holiday). The IRS periods are uneven: the June payment covers April and May only, and the January payment covers September to December. Setting aside by calendar quarter is a simplification for saving, not for calculating what you owe.

Step 6: A 15-minute monthly close

  1. Download your bank and card statements for the month.
  2. Check every transaction is in the ledger with a category. Add anything missing.
  3. Make sure the ledger matches the bank: money in and money out for the month should agree with the statement. Investigate any difference now, while you remember.
  4. Tick off receipts and chase any that are missing.
  5. Look at the P&L: profit, margin and any unusual expense.
  6. At quarter end, move the tax set-aside.

When to move beyond a spreadsheet

A spreadsheet works best for cash-basis businesses with one owner and up to a few hundred transactions a month. Consider accounting software or a bookkeeper when you hire employees and run payroll, carry inventory, need to track who owes you (accrual accounting), collect sales tax or VAT in several places, or bring in partners or investors who need formal reports.

Frequently asked questions

Can I do bookkeeping for my small business in Excel?

Yes. For a sole proprietor or small business on a cash basis with a manageable number of transactions, a well-structured spreadsheet with a ledger, consistent categories and a monthly P&L is a perfectly workable bookkeeping system.

How much should I set aside for taxes as a sole proprietor?

Many US sole proprietors set aside 25–30% of profit, since self-employment tax alone is roughly 14% of profit before income tax. The right rate depends on your total income, deductions and location, so confirm it with an accountant.

What is the difference between bookkeeping and accounting?

Bookkeeping is recording and categorising transactions. Accounting uses those records to prepare financial statements, plan taxes and advise on decisions. Good bookkeeping makes accounting faster and cheaper.

What records should a small business keep?

Keep receipts and invoices for income and expenses, bank and card statements, and records supporting any deductions. Retention rules vary; in the US the IRS generally recommends keeping records for at least three years, and longer in some situations.

Should I use cash or accrual accounting?

Most very small businesses use the cash method: income is recorded when received and expenses when paid. It’s simpler and matches your bank account. Accrual accounting becomes useful when you carry inventory or have significant amounts owed to or by you.

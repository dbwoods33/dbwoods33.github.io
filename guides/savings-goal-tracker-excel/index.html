How to track multiple savings goals and sinking funds

Most budget-busting expenses aren’t emergencies. They’re bills we knew were coming and didn’t save for. Sinking funds turn them into small monthly amounts, and a simple tracker keeps them all straight.

The short version

  • A sinking fund is money set aside monthly for a known future cost: insurance, gifts, car repairs, a trip.
  • For each goal: needed per month = (target − saved) ÷ months left. Recalculate whenever something changes.
  • You don’t need a separate bank account per goal. One savings account plus a ledger that tags each deposit to a goal works well.
  • Check “on track” by comparing what you’ve saved with what you should have saved by now.

An annual car insurance premium, a holiday season, a set of tyres, a friend’s wedding. None of these are surprises, yet they wreck budgets every year because they arrive as one large bill instead of twelve small ones.

Sinking funds fix that. You decide what’s coming, divide it by the months you have, and set that amount aside every month. When the bill arrives, the money is already there. This guide shows how to plan them, how to run several at once without a dozen bank accounts, and how to tell at a glance which goals are falling behind.

Sinking funds vs an emergency fund vs savings goals

  • Emergency fund: money for things you can’t predict, such as job loss or a medical bill. It has no due date and you hope never to use it.
  • Sinking funds: money for things you can predict but that don’t happen monthly. You fully expect to spend them.
  • Savings goals: bigger targets with a chosen date, like a house deposit or a new car. They work exactly like sinking funds, only larger.

Keeping them separate matters. If your emergency fund also pays for Christmas, you’ll find it half empty when a real emergency arrives.

Step 1: List what’s coming

Go through the last twelve months of bank and card statements and note every expense that wasn’t monthly. Common sinking funds include:

  • Insurance premiums paid annually or every six months
  • Car repairs, servicing, tyres and registration
  • Holidays, birthdays and gifts
  • Travel and vacations
  • Annual subscriptions and memberships
  • Medical and dental costs not covered by insurance
  • Home maintenance and replacing appliances
  • School fees, uniforms and activities

For each one, write down a target amount, the date you need it, and anything you’ve already saved toward it.

Step 2: Work out the monthly amount for each goal

The formula is the same for every goal:

Needed per month = (Target − Saved so far) ÷ Months left

Needed per month = (target − saved so far) ÷ months left.

Five goals that would have been $5,680 of “unexpected” bills become a single $500 monthly transfer. That is the whole idea.

In a spreadsheet with the target in B2, saved in C2 and due date in D2, calculate months left and the monthly amount like this:

Months left:  =MAX(1,(YEAR(D2)-YEAR(TODAY()))*12+MONTH(D2)-MONTH(TODAY()))
Per month:    =MAX(0,(B2-C2)/E2)

The MAX(1, …) stops a divide-by-zero in the month a goal is due, and MAX(0, …) shows zero once a goal is fully funded.

If the total is more than you can afford

Adjust in this order: push back dates on flexible goals (vacation), lower targets on optional ones, and only then trim the essentials. Never just skip a month without recalculating; the shortfall has to be made up somewhere.

Step 3: One account, many goals

You have two good options:

  • Separate accounts or “buckets.” Many banks let you split one savings account into named pots. It’s visual and hard to raid by accident, but awkward if your bank doesn’t offer it.
  • One account plus a ledger. Keep all sinking fund money in one high-interest savings account, and record every deposit and withdrawal in a spreadsheet with a Goal column. Each goal’s balance is =SUMIFS(Amount, Goal, "Holiday gifts"), with withdrawals entered as negative amounts.

The ledger approach scales to any number of goals, and the total of all goal balances should always equal your account balance. If it doesn’t, something wasn’t logged.

Step 4: Check whether each goal is on track

A progress bar tells you how far you’ve come. It doesn’t tell you whether you’re keeping pace. For that, compare what you’ve saved with what you should have saved by now if you’d saved evenly:

Expected by now = Target × (Months elapsed ÷ Total months)

Example: a $2,400 vacation fund started on 1 January, due at the end of December. After five months you should have $2,400 × 5 ÷ 12 = $1,000. If you have $850, you’re $150 behind. Spreading that shortfall over the remaining seven months raises the monthly amount from $200 to $221.43.

A simple status rule works well: Reached if saved ≥ target, On track if saved ≥ expected, otherwise Behind.

Step 5: Spend from the fund without guilt

Sinking funds are meant to be spent. When the insurance bill arrives, pay it from the fund, log the withdrawal, and start again for the next one. If the bill comes in higher than expected, cover the gap from flexible goals rather than from your emergency fund, then raise next year’s target.

Automate the boring part

Set up one standing transfer from checking to savings for the monthly total, timed for the day after payday so the money moves before it can be spent. Then, once a month, split that deposit across your goals in the ledger: $150 to car insurance, $80 to gifts and so on. One transfer is easier to keep up than five, and the ledger still shows exactly what each goal holds. If you’re paid every two weeks, divide the monthly total in two and transfer half each payday.

Withdrawals are also when trackers usually break: a goal reaches 100%, money comes out, and the progress bar never moves again. Make sure your tracker counts withdrawals so balances stay honest.

Where to keep the money

Sinking fund money should be safe and easy to reach, because you will spend it within months. A high-yield savings account at an insured bank is the usual choice: it earns some interest and keeps the money out of your everyday checking account. Avoid investing money you need within a year or two, since a market dip could leave a fund short just when the bill arrives.

Which goals to fund first

  1. A starter emergency fund, even one month of essentials.
  2. Near-term, non-negotiable bills: insurance, registration, tax.
  3. Predictable but flexible costs: gifts, car maintenance.
  4. Wants with flexible dates: travel, upgrades.

Frequently asked questions

What is a sinking fund?

A sinking fund is money you set aside each month for a specific expense you know is coming, such as an annual insurance premium or holiday gifts, so you can pay it in full when it arrives.

How many sinking funds should I have?

Start with three to five covering your biggest irregular costs. You can add more once the habit sticks. Too many small funds make tracking tedious.

Should sinking funds be in separate bank accounts?

They don’t need to be. One savings account plus a ledger that tags each deposit to a goal works well, as long as the total of your goals matches the account balance.

Is a sinking fund the same as an emergency fund?

No. An emergency fund covers unpredictable events and ideally stays untouched. Sinking funds cover predictable expenses and are meant to be spent.

How do I calculate how much to save each month?

Subtract what you’ve already saved from the target, then divide by the number of months until you need the money. Recalculate whenever the target, date or balance changes.

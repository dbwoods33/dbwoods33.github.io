Wedding budget breakdown by category, with percentages

Start with a total and a guest count, split it by percentage, then track quotes and deposits against the plan. Here’s a sensible starting split, and where real weddings usually go over.

The short version

  • Venue and catering together typically take about half the budget; this guide’s starting split puts them at 26% and 24%.
  • Guest count is the biggest lever: catering, chairs, stationery and favors all scale per person.
  • Track three numbers per category: allocated, forecast (your quote or contract) and paid.
  • Read every quote for service charges, tax, gratuities and overtime, and keep a contingency.

Wedding budgets don’t usually fail because of one extravagant decision. They fail through a series of reasonable ones: a slightly bigger venue, a few more guests, an upgraded menu, each fine on its own and together several thousand over.

The fix is to decide how the money splits before you start booking, then measure every quote against that split. This guide gives you a starting breakdown by category, shows what it looks like at three budget levels, and covers how to keep it on track through a year of deposits.

Start with two numbers: total budget and guest count

Total budget is what you can actually spend: your savings, what you can save before the date, and any confirmed family contributions. Get contributions agreed in writing, or at least in a clear conversation, before you plan around them.

Guest count shapes almost every other number. Catering is priced per head; so are chairs, place settings, cake, favors and invitations. Venues come in capacity bands. Settle on a realistic range, say 100 to 120, before you visit venues.

A few notes on each line:

  • Venue (26%) covers the ceremony and reception space. Some venues include tables, chairs, linen and staff; others are an empty room. Compare like with like.
  • Catering (24%) covers food, drink, staff and usually cake. This is where per-head costs and add-ons pile up.
  • Photography and video (11%) is one of the few things you keep. Couples who cut it hard often regret it.
  • Flowers and decor (8%) is the most flexible category. Seasonal flowers and venue-provided decor go a long way.
  • Attire (7%) includes alterations and accessories, not just the dress or suit. Alterations alone can run to hundreds.
  • Rings (6%) means the wedding bands; engagement rings are usually budgeted separately.
  • Honeymoon (5%). If it’s funded separately, move this share to contingency.
  • Other (2%): officiant, licence fees, tips and small extras.

Every wedding is different. A destination wedding, a backyard wedding or a big family celebration will shift these numbers dramatically. The value of a split is not that it’s “right”. It’s that you decide your priorities up front: if photography matters more to you than flowers, move the percentage deliberately instead of drifting.

Think per guest

At $30,000 with 120 guests, catering at 24% is $7,200, which is $60 per guest. Venue and catering together are $15,000, or $125 per guest.

That per-guest figure is the clearest way to see the trade-off on your guest list. Cutting 20 guests at $60 a head saves $1,200 on catering alone, before stationery, favors, rentals and possibly a smaller venue. Adding 20 guests costs at least the same.

When you get catering quotes, divide by your guest count and compare the per-head price to your allocation. It’s an immediate check on whether a menu fits.

Move percentages to match your priorities

Once you have the starting split, rank what matters most to you and move money deliberately. Suppose photography is your top priority and favors aren’t. On a $30,000 budget, raising photography from 11% to 14% adds $900, taking it from $3,300 to $4,200. Fund it by dropping favors from 1.5% to zero ($450) and trimming flowers and decor from 8% to 6.5% ($2,400 to $1,950, another $450). The total stays at 100%, and the trade-off is a decision you made rather than one that happened to you.

Do this before you visit vendors. It’s far easier to say no to an upgrade when you’ve already decided where that money is going.

The costs couples forget

Quotes often show a base price. The final invoice can be much higher. Ask every vendor whether their quote includes:

  • Service charges and gratuities. Catering and venue service charges can add a fifth or more to a quote.
  • Sales tax, which may apply on top of the service charge.
  • Overtime for photographers, musicians and venues if the night runs long.
  • Delivery, setup and breakdown fees for rentals, flowers and cake.
  • Cake cutting, corkage or outside-vendor fees charged by some venues.
  • Alterations for attire.
  • Vendor meals for the photographer, band or coordinator who works through dinner.
  • Postage for invitations, which costs more for heavy or oversized cards.

Because these add up, keep a contingency of around 5% if you can. The starting split above only has 2% in “Other”, so the simplest move is to trim flowers, favors or the honeymoon line and park the difference as a buffer.

Track allocated, forecast and paid

Once you start booking, one number per category isn’t enough. Track three:

  • Allocated: your plan, from the percentage split.
  • Forecast: what it will actually cost, using the estimate until you sign and the contract price after.
  • Paid: deposits and instalments so far.

Forecast minus allocated tells you if a category is over before you’ve paid for it, while you can still make a different choice. Forecast minus paid tells you how much you still owe, which is the number that matters for cash flow.

Schedule every deposit and balance

Most vendors take a deposit at booking and the balance close to the date. That means a large share of the money leaves in the last month or two, often while you’re also paying for attire, hair trials and travel. List every payment with its due date as soon as you sign. A simple “due in the next 30 days” view will save you from late fees and last-minute panic.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of a wedding budget goes to the venue?

A common starting point is around a quarter of the budget for the venue, with catering taking a similar share. Together they are often about half. Venues that include catering or rentals change the split, so compare total packages.

How do I split a $30,000 wedding budget?

Using the starting split in this guide: about $7,800 venue, $7,200 catering, $3,300 photography and video, $2,400 flowers and decor, $2,100 attire, $1,800 rings, $1,500 honeymoon and the rest across music, stationery, beauty, transport, favors and other costs.

What is the biggest wedding expense?

For most weddings it’s the venue and catering together, largely because catering scales with the guest count. Reducing guests is usually the most effective way to cut costs.

How much should I keep as a wedding contingency?

Around 5% is a sensible buffer for service charges, overtime, alterations and last-minute extras. If your plan doesn’t have room, trim a flexible category like decor or favors.

When are wedding vendor payments due?

Typically a deposit at booking and the balance shortly before the wedding, sometimes with an instalment in between. Check each contract and put every due date in your tracker as soon as you sign.
